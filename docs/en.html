History

Native Americans are the oldest inhabitants of the North American continent. Their ancestors were hunter-gatherer communities who crossed from Asia to America via the Bering Strait approximately 15,000–20,000 years ago. Over time they spread to every corner of the continent and developed hundreds of distinct peoples, languages and cultures.

The European contact that began with Christopher Columbus's voyage in 1492 was a turning point in the history of the native peoples. Diseases, wars and land losses greatly reduced their populations; however, their cultures continue to live on today. In the United States and Canada today, more than 5 million people identify themselves as of native descent.

Major Tribes

Lakota (Sioux)

The famous mounted hunters of the Great Plains. Their resistance against the US Army, led by figures like Sitting Bull and Red Cloud, has become legendary.

One of the most populous tribes in America. Famous for weaving and sand painting art; the "Long Walk" tragedy marked their history.

Pre-Columbian Civilizations

Giant earthen mound cities like Cahokia were the great cities of pre-European North America.

Culture and Belief

  • Respect for the land: Nature is a mother; every hunted animal is thanked.
  • Totem poles: The carving art telling family lineage among Pacific coast tribes.
  • Medicine Wheel: Symbolizes the four directions, the four seasons and the balance of life.
  • Kachina spirits: Representatives of rain and abundance of the Hopi and Zuni peoples.
  • Ghost Dance: A spiritual movement representing hope in 1890.
  • Oral art: Storytelling is the most valuable form of passing on heritage.

Ritual Dances

Native American dances are not entertainment — they are prayers and bonds of community. Every dance has a purpose, a story and its own season. Here are the most important ones:

Snake Dance (Hopi)

One of the most mysterious ceremonies used by the Hopi people in their rain prayers. Dancers dance with live snakes in their hands and release them into nature at the end of the ceremony. The photo dates from 1900.

📷 Public domain

Sun Dance

The most sacred ceremony of the Plains tribes (Lakota, Crow, Cheyenne). It lasts 4 days in midsummer; dancers dance for long hours to purify their spirits and pray for their communities. Banned by the US for a century; today it has been revived.

📷 Crow Sun Dance, ~1900 · Public domain

Ghost Dance

A spiritual movement started in 1889 by the Paiute prophet Wovoka. Tribes danced in circles hand in hand, hoping for the return of a peaceful world. White settlers misunderstood and feared this dance; its suppression led to the Wounded Knee massacre in 1890.

Rain Dance

A ceremonial dance among Southwestern tribes (Pueblo, Hopi, Zuni) to summon rain in times of drought. Dancers imitate the fall of rain with feathers and rhythmic steps; the sound of drums symbolizes thunder.

Traditional Festivals and Ceremonies

Powwow (Pow-wow)

Today's liveliest native tradition! Hundreds of powwows are held throughout the year. Tribal members dance in colorful traditional attire, drummers sing, and handicrafts and food are exhibited. Today it is a celebration open to both natives and visitors.

Historic Powwow (1947)

A genuine frame from the 1947 powwow in Onion Lake, Canada — proof of how the tradition has been carried from generation to generation.

📷 Free license

Harvest Ceremonies

Ceremonies held to celebrate the autumn harvest. Among the Iroquois, the Thanksgiving Address lasts all day; gratitude is expressed for every living thing. These ceremonies are said to have inspired the American Thanksgiving Day.

Coming-of-Age Ceremonies

In the Navajo Kinaalda ceremony girls step into adulthood; the Apache Sunrise Dance lasts 4 days. In Plains tribes, a feast is held in the family's honor when young people make their first hunt.

The Native Calendar: 13 Moons

Many tribes used a 13-moon calendar instead of the 365-day calendar. Each phase of the moon counted as a "moon" and each moon had a name. The flow of the year was intertwined with nature — marked by the transitions of the seasons:

  • Spring (Moon of Equalness): Sugar maple gathering, spring prayers
  • Summer (Strawberry Moon / Corn Moon): Sun Dance, strawberry festival
  • Autumn (Harvest Moon / Hunter's Moon): Harvest ceremonies, bison hunts
  • Winter (Snow Moon / Cold Moon): Storytelling season — in winter the spirits speak

Moon names vary between tribes — such as "Moon When the Outsiders Come" among the Lakota, or "Windy Moon" among the Cherokee.

Origins and the Asian Connection

Paleoanthropological and genetic research shows that the ancestors of North America's first peoples came from Siberia and the vast Asian steppes. Approximately 15,000 to 20,000 years ago, during a period when the Bering Strait was frozen, hunter-gatherer communities crossed this passage and set foot on the new continent.

Some inherited and cultural traces of these early migrants still parallel the peoples of Altai, Siberia and the Far East today. Mitochondrial DNA studies have shown that Native Americans descend from a single genetic pool (similar to the ancient Mal'ta-Buret culture in Siberia).

The Siberian Connection Ancient DNA analyses show that the first migrants shared the same genetic lines as hunter-gatherer groups in southern Siberia.
Beringia: The Lost Land Beringia, the vast landmass that appeared when the Bering Strait froze during the Ice Age, formed a migration corridor between Asia and America for thousands of years.
Language and Culture Parallels Anthropologists record similar motifs between some Native American beliefs, totemism, shamanism and sky cults, and the peoples of Siberia/Altai.
Three Major Migration Waves According to linguistic and archaeological evidence, there were at least three major migration waves to North America: the first 15-20 thousand years ago, the second 8-10 thousand years ago (Na-Dene languages), and the third the Eskimo-Aleut migration (5 thousand years ago).

Note: Establishing a direct line of descent between Native Americans and modern Turkic peoples is not scientifically accepted; however, both peoples share a broad hunter-gatherer heritage stemming from the deep history of the Asian steppes.

Resistance and Historical Events

Against the loss of land, forced migration and assimilation policies that began with the arrival of Europeans, native peoples resisted for centuries. This resistance took shape across a wide spectrum, from armed struggle to legal and cultural struggle.

Proclamation of 1763 King George III of England promised to leave the lands west of the Appalachian Mountains to the native peoples. However, the colonies did not recognize this principle.
War of 1812 and Tecumseh Shawnee leader Tecumseh tried to establish a great native confederation against the USA. He was killed in battle, but his legacy lives on.
Little Bighorn (1876) Lakota and Cheyenne warriors defeated the US 7th Cavalry Regiment under Colonel Custer. This victory became the symbol of native resistance.
Wounded Knee Massacre (1890) The tragedy in which the US army killed more than 300 Lakota civilians to suppress the Ghost Dance. It is seen as the symbolic end of armed resistance.
Wounded Knee Occupation (1973) The American Indian Movement (AIM) symbolically occupied the lands of the 1890 massacre, drawing the world's attention to native rights.
Standing Rock (2016) The peaceful resistance by the Lakota and their supporters against the Dakota Access Pipeline became one of the most important actions of today's native rights struggle.

Mythology and Legends

The mythology of Native American peoples is a rich world intertwined with nature, ruled by animal spirits and heroes. These stories have been passed down from generation to generation.

🐺 The Wolf and Creation

For many Native Americans, the wolf is a symbol of creation and rebirth. According to Lakota mythology, the first humans fell from the universe as wolves and then turned back into humans.

🦅 The Eagle and the Skies

The eagle is known as the Son of the Skies and is one of the most sacred animals. Its feathers carry courage and honor when added to war bonnets. Eagle dreams are the most sublime messages.

🐻 The Bear Doctor

In many tribes, the bear is the holder of healing knowledge. According to Cherokee legend, the first humans learned the healing of plants from bears. The bear's hibernation symbolizes death and resurrection.

🦌 The White Buffalo Calf Woman

In Lakota legend, the White Buffalo Calf Woman is a sacred prophetess. She came with a white buffalo calf and brought the peace pipe to the People. The birth of a white buffalo is a sacred sign.

🌊 Water Monsters

In Iroquois legend, the Thunderer is a giant bird. When it flaps its wings, storms and lightning are born. The water monster Mitla is also found at the source of the Mississippi.

🌙 The Ghost Dance

A spiritual movement started in 1890 by the Paiute leader Wovoka. White-clad dancers believed that the land would return and the ancestors would come back to life.

The Role of Women and Famous Figures

Although the place of women in Native American societies varies from tribe to tribe, many communities had strong autonomy and respect for women. In matrilineal systems, descent is traced through the mother; land and home belonged to women.

Sacagawea (1788-1812) Of the Shoshone tribe. She served as a guide and interpreter on the Lewis and Clark expedition. Knowing the mountain passes was critical to the expedition's success.
Pocahontas (1596-1617) Of the Powhatan tribe. She acted as an intermediary in peace negotiations with the English colony of Jamestown. The legend of her saving John Smith's life became popular in Western culture.
Wilma Mankiller (1945-2010) The first female principal chief of the Cherokee Nation. She served between 1985-1995, revitalized the tribal economy and improved education.
Maria Tallchief (1925-2013) Of the Osage tribe. America's first great ballerina. She starred as prima ballerina of the New York City Ballet for 13 years.
Matoaka (Pocahontas's real name) Her name in the Powhatan tribe means "Playful One." Her father Powhatan ruled the strongest confederation in the Virginia region.

Sacred Sites

🏔️ Black Hills (Paha Sapa) For the Lakota, "The Place Where the Heart Becomes Everything." Declared to be protected "forever" by the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty, but broken when gold was discovered in 1874. Today the world's largest mountain carving, the Crazy Horse Memorial, is being built here (172 meters when complete).
🏔️ Devils Tower (Mato Tipila) Means "Bear Lodge." This 264-meter tower in Wyoming is sacred to more than 20 tribes. According to legend, when girls being chased by two bear brothers climbed here, God raised the tower upward.
🏜️ Monument Valley (Tsé Biiʼ Ndzisgaii) The sacred lands of the Navajo people. Means "Rocks in the Valley." Every tower hosts a legend: the Mittens are the hands of the First People. John Ford's 7 western films were shot here (1939-1964).
🌲 Redwood Forests Sacred areas of the Yurok, Karok and Tolowa peoples. The 2,000-year-old redwood trees are seen as "living ancestors."
🏞️ Bear Butte (Mato Paha) A sacred hill in South Dakota. A spiritual center where Vision Quests are performed. A motocross track plan in the 1970s was blocked by the occupation of Lakota leaders in 1982.
⛰️ Mount Shasta A 4,322-meter volcano, the highest point of Northern California. In Modoc legend, the First People filled the world by fishing here. For the Karuk people, it is where the souls of salmon live.

Famous Leaders

Red Cloud (1822–1909)

Oglala Lakota leader. The only native commander to win a war against the US Army; he led to the closure of the Fort Phil Kearny forts.

📷 Public domain

Chief Joseph (1840–1904)

Nez Perce leader. The famous retreat in which he led his people 1,600 km against the US Army is still studied in military history lessons. The saying "I was born to live free, not to fight" belongs to him.

📷 E.S. Curtis · Public domain

Sacagawea (c. 1788–1812)

Shoshone woman guide. She interpreted and guided the Lewis and Clark expedition across the continent; she is one of America's most famous female figures.

📷 Monument statue · Public domain

Sequoyah (c. 1770–1843)

Cherokee scholar. He single-handedly invented the Cherokee syllabary — one of the rare examples in history of a language owing its writing to one person. The giant sequoia trees bear his name.

📷 H. Inman painting · Public domain

Native Peoples Today

Native culture is not a history confined to museums and books — it is alive today. In the United States there are 574 federally recognized tribes, and in Canada there are more than 600 First Nations communities.

  • Their own lands: Reservations have their own governments, schools and courts.
  • Language revival: Schools and apps have been established to teach languages like Navajo and Cherokee again.
  • Powwow: Traditional dance, music and clothing are displayed at annual tribal gatherings.
  • Art and cinema: Native artists, writers and actors are becoming increasingly visible on the world stage.
  • Defending land rights: Native leaders are also cited as leading voices against the climate crisis.
